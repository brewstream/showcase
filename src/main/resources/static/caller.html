Calling out to a source, and reading what it actually delivers
Listener Caller

Several sources at once is the point — connect to as many as you like. They share one event loop group rather than a thread pool each. Latency is the recovery budget for this path, in milliseconds: roughly three to four times its round-trip time.

The other half of SRT. The listener page waits to be published to and puts a lossy relay in front of itself, because a loopback never drops anything and recovery has nothing to recover from. This page does the opposite: it connects out to a real source over a real path, and there is nothing to induce — whatever the network does is what the figures show.

Nothing below the connection changed. The cards here are rendered by the same code as the listener page, from the same JSON, because Roast gives a connection the same type whichever end opened it and Grind never learns which end it is attached to. Attaching the analysis is the same two handlers in the same order:

connection.pipeline().addLast(new MpegTsDecoder(analyzer), new TsHealthHandler(analyzer));

Reading the result. Against a source across a real network, the figures worth watching first are dropped and errored seconds. Lost-but-recovered packets cost nothing visible and are the normal state of a working link. Dropped packets are what ARQ could not retrieve inside the latency budget, and they land as continuity errors on a named track moments later. If dropped is climbing, the latency budget is too small for the path's round-trip time — that is the number to raise, not the bitrate to lower.

The budget is per connection, because it belongs to the path rather than to the application. A local encoder is happy at 40ms; a source several hops away needs three to four times its round-trip time, and the RTT figure on each card is what to multiply. Connect to the same source twice at two different settings and the trade-off is on screen side by side: the longer budget recovers more and shows fewer errored seconds, and is further behind live for it.

The listener page runs at a deliberately low 30ms so its loss slider has a reachable breaking point on loopback. That figure would be wrong here, and this page does not share it.

Slack is the smallest gap a track ever left between a timestamp and the clock — how much time the decoder had in hand before that frame was due. Audio routinely runs tighter than video — around 100 ms is normal — so this only turns amber below 50 ms, where the margin is genuinely thin. Nothing here goes red on a stream that is merely close to the line; late frames counts the ones that actually missed, which a player can only stall on or drop.

Read slack down a column over time, not across tracks. Video and audio sit at quite different figures by nature — around 720 ms and 410 ms in a typical ffmpeg stream — because they are buffered and interleaved differently. That difference is not lip-sync drift, and treating it as one would condemn every working stream.

Ad markers appear only when the stream signals them, on a PID the PMT declares as SCTE-35. That PID is silent between breaks — minutes of nothing is the normal state, not a fault.

Each marker shows two times, because they are different things. Received is when the signal reached us; fires is the moment it describes, which is always later. The gap between them — the warning — is how much notice a downstream ad server got, a couple of seconds being typical. A warning that arrives after the moment it describes could not have been acted on, and is called out as such rather than shown as zero.

Markers are sent more than once for redundancy, so repeated copies are folded into one row with a count rather than listed as separate breaks.

PCR spacing is the widest gap between consecutive clock references, and it will almost certainly read 80 ms in amber against anything ffmpeg produced. That is real — ETSI TR 101 290 asks for one every 40 ms, and ffmpeg's muxer defaults to twice that — but it is a conformance finding, not damage. Nothing is lost; the receiver's clock recovery just has less to work with. It is shown apart from the health badge for exactly that reason, and the badge stays green. Add -pcr_period 20 to ffmpeg and it goes away.

Failures are kept on screen rather than cleared, because why a connection did not establish is usually the most useful thing this page can tell you. A wrong passphrase is reported as such; so is a source that never answered, which almost always means the address, the port, or a firewall.

On the passphrase. It is sent over whatever this page is served on, which in a demo is plain HTTP, and it is bound as a char[] and zeroed as soon as Roast has copied it, so this application never holds a String of it that would sit in the heap until GC. That is a real precaution and a small one: point this at your own encoder on your own network, and do not treat it as a credential store.
