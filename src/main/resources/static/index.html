What to watch. Transport loss and media damage are different things. Packets lost are usually recovered by retransmission and cost nothing visible. Packets dropped are ones ARQ could not recover inside the latency budget — those are gone, and they land as continuity errors on a specific track a moment later.

Try this. At 5%, watch lost and recovered climb together while dropped and the continuity errors stay at zero. Every lost packet is being retransmitted and arriving in time, and the media is untouched. That is SRT working, and it is the more interesting half of the demonstration. Push the slider up until dropped moves — that is the budget running out, and a continuity error lands on a named track moments later.

Errored seconds is the column a broadcast engineer would look at first. It is ETSI TR 101 290's measure: how many seconds of stream time contained at least one error, counted once however many errors the second holds. Forty continuity errors in one bad second is one errored second, and that is the point — it answers for how long was this stream broken rather than how many packets went wrong, which is much closer to what someone watching would describe.

The clock is the stream's own PCR, not this server's. A file pushed through faster than real time still reports the seconds it actually contains.

GOP damage is the same events counted by media structure instead of by time, and it is the column closest to what a viewer saw. A GOP runs between two points where a decoder could start — keyframes — and everything in it depends on the frame that begins it, so one gap anywhere ruins all of it. Two packets lost inside one GOP is a single glitch; two spread across two GOPs is two. The two columns disagree in a way worth watching: a burst of loss inside one second damages one GOP, while the same loss spread thinly damages every GOP it touches while marking the same one second.

Read GOP damage on video. Nearly every audio frame is its own starting point, so damage there is a click rather than a ruined second, and the column degenerates into an error count.

Ad markers appear only when the stream signals them, on a PID the PMT declares as SCTE-35. That PID is silent between breaks — minutes of nothing is the normal state, not a fault.

Each marker shows two times, because they are different things. Received is when the signal reached us; fires is the moment it describes, which is always later. The gap between them — the warning — is how much notice a downstream ad server got, a couple of seconds being typical. A warning that arrives after the moment it describes could not have been acted on, and is called out as such rather than shown as zero.

Markers are sent more than once for redundancy, so repeated copies are folded into one row with a count rather than listed as separate breaks.

PCR spacing is the widest gap between consecutive clock references, and it will almost certainly read 80 ms in amber against anything ffmpeg produced. That is real — ETSI TR 101 290 asks for one every 40 ms, and ffmpeg's muxer defaults to twice that — but it is a conformance finding, not damage. Nothing is lost; the receiver's clock recovery just has less to work with. It is shown apart from the health badge for exactly that reason, and the badge stays green. Add -pcr_period 20 to ffmpeg and it goes away.

Recovered counts packets that arrived carrying the retransmit flag. Retransmit rate is not shown because it is a send-side figure and this side only receives: it would read zero however hard ARQ was working.

Loss is applied in both directions, so the NAKs asking for a retransmission and the ACKs confirming arrival are as exposed as the data. Recovery has to survive losing its own signalling. The measured figure beside the slider is the relay's own count — a slider claiming 10% is worth nothing next to proof that 10% went missing.
